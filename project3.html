Project Overview

This project explores how augmented reality (AR) can support students who struggle to stay focused during lectures or who face language barriers in class. We designed an AR-enhanced lecture experience that provides visual cues, key-concept highlights, and language support on top of the existing classroom environment.

Our target audience includes:

  • Professors who want to keep students engaged and check understanding in real time.
  • Students with attention and focus difficulties (for example, ADHD-like symptoms).
  • Students with lower English proficiency who benefit from subtitles and visual scaffolding.

My Role

I worked mainly on the interview and Point-of-View (POV) / opportunity framing for this project. I helped design and conduct interviews with both teachers and students, synthesized their responses into concrete POV statements, and translated these into opportunity areas for our final AR solution.

Because I personally struggle with staying focused in lectures, I was also an “internal user.” This made it easier for me to recognize when our ideas truly addressed real attention problems and when they were only “nice to have” features.

Design Process

1. Needfinding: Interviews with Teachers and Students

Together with my teammates, I interviewed professors about when students seem most disengaged in class and which types of content are hardest to teach. I also interviewed students about where they lose focus, what distracts them, and how they currently try to keep up with fast lectures. For this part of the project I was responsible for the interview questions and the interview POP section.

From these conversations we identified three primary user groups:

  • Professors who need a way to keep students engaged and make sure core concepts are clearly understood.
  • Student A – focus difficulties, who misses important points during long lectures and needs structured, visual cues to stay on track.
  • Student B – lower English proficiency, who struggles with terminology and speed and needs multilingual or visual support.

2. Point-of-View Statements & Mindmap

Based on the interviews, I helped write POV statements for each user type and co-created a mindmap that connected “focus” to potential AR features. These features included subtitles, progress bars, visual emphasis of key ideas, and language translation overlays.

3. Concept Storyboards & Speed-Dating

As a team we sketched several storyboards, such as AR-enhanced classroom lectures, VR labs, discussion forums, and onsite VR field trips. We then used a “speed-dating” method to quickly test these concepts with potential users and gathered feedback on which scenarios felt most valuable and realistic for everyday use.

The results clearly showed that AR enhancements for in-person classroom lectures were the most preferred direction, because they directly support existing classes instead of replacing them.

4. Final AR Concept & Evaluation

We converged on an AR system that overlays:

  • Key-concept highlights and visual explanations during lectures.
  • Subtitles or simplified text for students with language barriers.
  • Focus-supporting elements such as progress bars and subtle notifications.

After creating a prototype and showing it to participants, we collected survey data on usability and learning impact. Most participants reported that the AR overlays were easy to follow and significantly improved their understanding of the lecture content.

Personal Reflection

This project helped me connect my own learning challenges with a broader design problem. I have a hard time maintaining focus during long lectures; I often miss small but important details and then feel lost when the topic moves on. While interviewing students with similar issues, I realized that my experience is not unique – many classmates silently struggle in the same way. This made the project feel very personal and kept me motivated to design something that would genuinely help us.

Working on the interview and POV section taught me how powerful good needfinding can be. At first, our ideas about AR in education were very general and almost “sci-fi.” After talking with teachers and students, I learned to frame the problem more precisely: professors need to maintain engagement without extra workload, while students need real-time, lightweight support that fits inside the existing classroom flow. Translating raw interview notes into clear POV statements was challenging, but it forced me to prioritize which needs were truly critical.

I also noticed how my own attention issues influenced the features I pushed for. For example, I strongly supported progress bars, visual emphasis on key moments, and subtle notifications for when an important concept started. These came directly from thinking about the moments when my mind drifts and what could gently bring it back without overwhelming me. In this sense, I was both a designer and a user, and I had to be careful to check my personal biases against actual interview data.

Throughout the project I used AI tools mainly to brainstorm question wording, clean up survey text, and organize notes into themes. However, the final interpretations and design decisions were made by our team. This experience showed me that AI can speed up some mechanical parts of the process, but deep understanding still requires human judgment and empathy.

Looking forward, I would like to explore how this AR system could adapt to different kinds of attention problems and languages in a more personalized way. For example, the interface could slowly reduce the amount of support as a student becomes more confident, or it could highlight concepts differently depending on the student’s learning style. Overall, this project made me more aware of the invisible struggles that happen in a classroom and how thoughtful interaction design can make learning more inclusive for everyone, including myself.
